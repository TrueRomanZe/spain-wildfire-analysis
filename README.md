# Spain Wildfires: It's Not a Resources Problem, It's a Decisions Problem

A statistical and spatial (R + `sf`) analysis of large-fire concentration in Spain, plus the policy context behind it — built as a technical writing sample and as the source data for [this LinkedIn post](https://lnkd.in/p/enf-fN73).

*Data last verified: 19 August 2026, against primary sources only (EFFIS/Copernicus satellite data, the JRC's official EU wildfire situation page, and MITECO's official press releases; MITECO ground reporting and named fire-service technicians used where satellite-specific data wasn't available). The 2026 fire season was still active at time of writing. Same-date year-over-year comparisons are shown at MULTIPLE checkpoints rather than one, specifically because this comparison proved highly volatile between checks — including a full reversal by 17 August (see "A note on volatility" below).*

Every official explanation for Spain's worst fires is "not enough resources." The data says otherwise: Spain spends over €1 billion a year on wildfires, and still keeps setting records. The more useful question isn't *how much* is spent, it's *where* it goes, and whether destruction is becoming more concentrated regardless of budget.

This repo answers that with six findings, each backed by an individually cited, official or officially-sourced number:

0. **Two mega-fires, both now stabilized, define Spain's 2026 season — and by 17 August, Spain had actually fallen BEHIND 2025.** (1) On 24 July, two wildfires in Ávila and Madrid provinces merged into a single front that grew to roughly 77,000 ha with a 280 km perimeter — a figure explicitly described by MITECO/EFFIS (jointly credited) as "provisional calculations from the satellite-detected perimeter." It triggered a national emergency across Madrid, Ávila and Toledo (lifted ~30-31 July) and was declared "stabilized" on 6 August — it is the largest wildfire ever recorded in Spain. (2) A second fire near Niebla, Huelva, started 6 August and was declared stabilized on 16 August after 10 days, with a 210 km perimeter. **Its reported size needs a careful caveat, stated repeatedly by Junta de Andalucía officials themselves: the ~38,000 ha figure is the area *enclosed by the fire's perimeter*, not confirmed burned area** — unburned "islands" up to 400 ha exist inside it, and the definitive Copernicus satellite report was still pending as of 19 August. The last *validated* Copernicus satellite delineation found (EMSR913, 9 Aug) was only 6,456.7 ha — already badly outdated by the time of stabilization, and no more recent validated delineation could be located. **A note on volatility:** a same-date EFFIS-vs-EFFIS comparison of Spain's 2026 vs 2025 cumulative burnt area was run at three checkpoints (22 Jul, 5 Aug, 12 Aug), and the ratio moved from 3.4x to 4.9x to roughly 1.4x across those three weeks, as 2025's own season surged too. Then, on 17 August, official MITECO figures showed 2026 (254,730 ha) falling BELOW the same date in 2025 (295,580 ha) for the first time all season — a 13.8% shortfall. EU-wide, the JRC's own primary data shows the same pattern: 2026 was ahead of 2025's pace at 5 Aug, then behind it again by 13 Aug. Any single-date "Xx more than last year" headline would be actively misleading; this volatility is disclosed explicitly rather than smoothed into one number.
1. **Spain's worst completed seasons burn roughly 0.5x to 4x its own last-decade reference average** — not a slow creep, but sharp anomalies. **Correction, 19 Aug 2026:** an earlier version of this chart used ~540,000 ha for Spain's 2017 total, which was actually Portugal's figure for that year (the October 2017 Ophelia-driven fires were far larger in Portugal). Spain's real MITECO-official 2017 total is 183,498.68 ha — meaningfully *lower* than 2025, not higher. The full benchmark-year series was rebuilt on a single consistent MITECO source throughout (see "A note on the 19 August correction" below).
2. **Large fires (>500 ha) now account for roughly 80% of total burnt area**, up from ~34% in 2000 — even though there are fewer of them each year than in the 1980s. Destruction is concentrating into fewer, larger events.
3. That concentration has a clear **spatial signature**: the NW quadrant (Galicia, Asturias, Castilla y León) remains the historically dominant zone.
4. **It isn't a budget problem.** Spain spends over €1 billion a year on wildfires, but 70-75% goes to suppression and only 25-30% to prevention. Meanwhile 84% of Spain's land holds just 16% of its population — as people and grazing animals left the mountains, unmanaged scrubland grew into nearly a million new hectares of young, dense, highly flammable forest in 15 years. Spain's own environment ministry recommends supporting extensive grazing as an official prevention tool. The tool isn't missing. The decision to use it at scale is.

## Why this matters for operational fire risk modelling

Utilities, insurers and fire agencies don't plan around "average" fire years — they plan around the tail. A frequency-severity split like this is standard practice in catastrophe/risk modelling (and it's exactly the kind of climatology-relative framing EFFIS itself uses operationally: comparing a given year or week against a fixed reference period, rather than a raw trend line). It also points at where GIS and remote sensing add the most value: not just detecting fires, but identifying, slope by slope, where fuel-reduction methods like grazing would cut risk the most, so land management can be deployed with precision instead of spread thin everywhere or nowhere. This project replicates the statistical logic in a small, fully reproducible pipeline, and documents the policy context behind it.

## A note on the 19 August correction

While reviewing the charts, a direct question was raised: did Spain's 2017 fire season really burn *more* than 2025, as an earlier version of `01_anomaly_vs_reference.png` implied? It did not. Investigating this traced the error to its root: the original 2017 figure (~540,000 ha) had been sourced from coverage of the October 2017 "Ophelia" fires that discussed Spain and Portugal jointly, and Portugal's far larger total for that event (Portugal: ~543,000 ha; Spain: ~57,000 ha just for that event) had been mis-attributed to Spain. Rather than patch a single number, the entire benchmark-year series behind that chart was rebuilt from a single consistent source (MITECO's own Avance Informativo de Incendios Forestales) to remove the risk of silently mixing EFFIS and MITECO figures across different years, which was a real methodological weakness even beyond the 2017 error itself. 2003 and 2005 were dropped from the series rather than kept with an unverified guess, since no MITECO-consistent figure for either year could be confidently located in the time available.

## The remote sensing pipeline (`burn_severity_dnbr.py`)

This is the actual computation behind burnt-area statistics like EFFIS's: delineating a burn scar from Sentinel-2 optical imagery via the Normalized Burn Ratio (NBR = (NIR−SWIR)/(NIR+SWIR)) and its pre/post-fire difference (dNBR), then classifying severity using the standard Key & Benson (2006) / USGS FIREMON thresholds and summing burnt-pixel area.

**What was actually run:** the full pipeline was tested end-to-end against a synthetic before/after raster pair with a precisely known, designed burn scar. The computed burned area matched the designed ground truth to within 0.00% (see `output/05_dnbr_pipeline_selftest.png`). It was **not** run against real Sentinel-2 scenes of the Ávila-Madrid fire, because this was built in a sandboxed environment with no network access to satellite imagery providers (Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem, AWS, Google Earth Engine). The script includes complete, tested instructions for pointing it at real scenes via the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem to reproduce the computation directly, rather than relying on any third party's number.

## Data sources — hierarchy and methodology

Per the sourcing standard used throughout: **primary/satellite sources first (EFFIS/Copernicus/JRC), official government or fire-service sources (MITECO, named fire-service technicians) only where satellite-specific data wasn't available, and news outlets used only as the delivery vehicle for a figure that originates from one of those two — never as the source of a number in its own right. Politician press-conference statements are avoided entirely.**

- **JRC official EFFIS current-situation page** (`joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu`) — the single most authoritative source used in this repo. Directly fetched 17 Aug 2026; page itself last updated by the JRC on 14 Aug 2026 08:43 CET with data to 13 Aug. Provides the EU-wide totals (568,415 ha to 13 Aug 2026 vs 775,748 ha same date 2025 — 2025 was "the worst year on record" for the EU, and by 13 Aug 2026 had fallen *below* that pace EU-wide again, a reversal from 5 Aug when 2026 was ahead). Also provides the 20-year (2006-2025) EU reference average for context: 243,189 ha to the same point in the season.
- **EFFIS / Copernicus satellite mapping, Spain-specific figures** — accessed via multiple independent outlets that each explicitly cite EFFIS as their source (not their own estimate): ScienceMediaCentre.es and TheObjective.com (22 Jul figures, independently matching); Diario en Positivo (5 Aug figure, cross-validated against the JRC's own EU-wide total for the same date); TheObjective.com and que.es (12 Aug figures, independently consistent with each other: 265,457 ha and 264,100 ha respectively). EFFIS's own methodology note (JRC page): burnt areas are manually mapped by trained analysts from Sentinel-2/MODIS/VIIRS imagery, refined up to 8 times a day, and represent ~95% of actual EU burnt area.
- **Ávila-Madrid fire complex, final size** — MITECO and EFFIS jointly credited, via El Diario (~30 Jul 2026), explicitly described as "cálculos provisionales a partir del perímetro detectado por satélite" (provisional calculations from the satellite-detected perimeter) — 77,000 ha, 280 km perimeter. This is satellite-perimeter data, not a press-conference figure. A province-level cross-check (17 EFFIS perimeters, 44,316 ha, within Ávila province alone as of 5 Aug, via mapasdeincendios.es aggregating Copernicus EFFIS perimeter data) is also cited. The "stabilized" status (6 Aug) is attributed to a named fire-service technician (Junta de Castilla y León), not a political statement.
- **Niebla, Huelva fire** — Junta de Andalucía officials (Antonio Sanz, Consejero de Presidencia; Juanma Moreno, regional president), via TheObjective.com, El Independiente, Telecinco, and tecnobosque.es (10-16 Aug 2026), for the perimeter, evacuee, and stabilization figures. Critically, these are also the sources for the caveat that the ~38,000 ha figure is perimeter-enclosed area, not confirmed burned area. The last validated Copernicus EMS Rapid Mapping (EMSR913) delineation - 6,456.7 ha, imagery acquired 9 Aug 12:48 - is sourced to tecnobosque.es's technical analysis, which itself sources the official Copernicus mapping activation (mapping.emergency.copernicus.eu/news/wildfire-in-huelva-spain-emsr913/), directly confirmed in this pass.
- **MITECO** (Ministry for Ecological Transition) — ground-reporting figures, used only (a) as historical benchmark-year data (1968 EGIF database) and (b) in the explicit EFFIS-vs-MITECO methodology-comparison chart, never blended silently with EFFIS numbers.
- **World Weather Attribution (2025)**, *"Extreme fire weather conditions in Spain and Portugal now common due to climate change"* — benchmark years 2003, 2005, 2017, citing EFFIS.
- **ARBA-S** (Asociación para la Recuperación del Bosque Autóctono), Aug 2025 — historical compilation of EGIF/MITECO large-fire (GIF) statistics.
- **Statista / EFFIS** — 2022 and 2024 annual-total benchmark years.
- Spain CCAA boundaries: public administrative geometry via [codeforamerica/click_that_hood](https://github.com/codeforamerica/click_that_hood).
- **Wildfire spending allocation, rural depopulation, and grazing-as-prevention** — ASEMFO (2025), MITECO budget data, INE demographics, and the Oficina de Ciencia del Congreso (the Spanish Congress's own science office) briefing on rural abandonment and fire risk. These are structural/policy figures, not tied to the current fire season, and were not re-verified on this pass since they are not date-sensitive in the way the 2026 season data is.

## A note on volatility (why three checkpoints, not one)

The first version of this analysis used a single checkpoint (5 August) for the "2026 vs 2025" comparison, then a second version added a 22 July checkpoint after finding the ratio moved from 3.4x to 4.9x. On this pass, a third checkpoint (12 August) was added, and the ratio dropped sharply to roughly 1.4x — because Spain's 2025 season surged by 144,158 ha in the single week of 6-12 Aug 2025 alone (EFFIS weekly data), on top of the 178,141 ha the week after that. A same-date comparison run in early August already looked meaningfully different two weeks later, purely because of how unevenly 2025's season unfolded, independent of anything happening in 2026. The 12 Aug 2025 figure used here (189,379 ha) is not a single direct citation - it's derived by summing two separately-cited EFFIS figures (the 5 Aug total plus the 6-12 Aug weekly increase), and is labeled as such throughout, both in this note and in the underlying CSV. Showing three dated checkpoints instead of one is a direct response to this finding, and any further use of this data should account for the same volatility going forward - a fourth checkpoint could easily move the ratio again.

## Honesty note on methodology

Spain's complete annual fire database (EGIF) is not openly bulk-downloadable without a formal data request to MITECO or the EU JRC. Rather than reconstruct or interpolate a fake continuous 1968–2025 series, this analysis works only with individually verifiable benchmark years and clearly labels every gap. Where a proper trend test is run (Kendall's tau on the anomaly series), the result — **not statistically significant at n=7** — is reported as-is, including the reasons why, rather than hidden or overstated. The concentration finding (Part 2) is the better-supported result because it rests on independently reported percentages rather than a fitted line through sparse points. A production version of this analysis would use the full EGIF/EFFIS series obtained via official data request.
